What to Build First in a Taxi App MVP: A Short List

The short list of features a first release needs to run real rides, and the longer list that can wait.

By TaxiAppDevelopment.org TeamPublished Nov 24, 2026Updated Nov 24, 2026 5 min read

On this page

Key takeaways

  • A first release needs to complete a ride safely, not to match a big competitor.
  • Passenger booking, driver job flow and a simple admin view are the core.
  • Promo codes, wallets and complex pricing can wait for later stages.
  • Launch in one place with real drivers, then let usage tell you what to add.

The goal of a first release

An MVP is not a small copy of a finished product. It is the smallest version that lets a real passenger book a real ride with a real driver, so you can learn whether the business works. Every feature you add before that point delays the learning and adds cost.

A useful test for each idea is simple: can a ride still happen safely without it? If yes, it is a candidate for later.

The must-haves

Three parts make a ride work, and each needs a minimum version.

The passenger side needs sign-up, a way to set pickup and drop-off, a clear fare or fare estimate, a booking confirmation and a way to see the driver arrive. The driver side needs sign-in, a way to go online, a job request they can accept, and a few tap-through stages from on the way to completed. The operator side needs a dashboard to see jobs and drivers, and a way to fix a problem by hand.

  • Passenger: sign-up, booking, fare estimate, live status.
  • Driver: online or offline, accept a job, update trip stages.
  • Admin: job list, driver list, manual assignment, basic reports.
  • Payments: one method that works well, even if it is only cash plus one card route.

What can wait

Many features feel essential and are not. They tend to be features that improve a business that already works.

Promo codes and referral rewards need customers first. Wallets need payment volume first. Surge pricing needs enough demand to make it fair. Multi-language and multi-currency need a second market. Corporate portals need corporate customers. Ratings and detailed reports matter more once there are enough trips to read.

  • Promo codes and referrals
  • In-app wallet
  • Dynamic or surge pricing
  • Several languages and currencies
  • Corporate accounts and invoicing
  • Advanced analytics

Writing the scope in plain language

Before design starts, write the first release as a short list of sentences, each starting with a person. A passenger can request a ride and see a fare estimate. A driver can accept a job and update its status. An administrator can see all jobs and assign a driver by hand.

If a feature cannot be written as a sentence about a person doing something, it is probably not clear enough to build. If the list is longer than about twenty sentences, the first release is probably too big.

Small does not mean rough

A minimum product still has to feel trustworthy. Passengers decide in seconds whether an app is reliable, and drivers decide whether it will cost them money. Put the effort into the few things that matter: a booking flow that is clear on a small phone, accurate arrival times, notifications that arrive, and a driver app that does not drop jobs.

It is better to have five features that work every time than fifteen that work most of the time. Reliability is what earns the second ride.

How to know it is working

Decide before launch what you will watch. Useful early measures are how many bookings are completed compared with those started, how long passengers wait, how many jobs drivers decline and how many rides need a manual fix.

These numbers tell you what to build next. If many bookings are abandoned at the payment step, improve payment. If jobs wait too long, improve dispatch. Let evidence set the order of the second stage.

Who needs to be involved

The first release is not only a technical project. Someone who runs the business should decide what matters, someone who drives should say what is practical, and someone who builds should say what is costly. Decisions made by only one of these groups are usually wrong in a way the others could have spotted.

Appoint one person to make the final call on scope. Without an owner, every request becomes a must-have and the first release grows quietly until it is no longer first.

Finally, agree how changes are handled. A short, written list of what is in, what is out and what is deferred makes it easy to say no to a good idea without losing it.

Start in one place

One city, or even one area, makes everything easier. Pricing is simpler, driver recruiting is local, support is manageable and you can fix problems by talking to people. A first release that works well in one place is a better base than a thin release spread across many.

Pick a place where you already have drivers or customers. If you run a taxi company, that is your current area. If you are a start-up, choose the area where you can recruit the first twenty drivers by hand.

Plan the second stage before you build the first

Keep a written list of what you left out and why. After launch, look at what real users ask for and where they get stuck, and move items from the list when real evidence supports them. This keeps decisions honest, and it stops the later stages from being driven by guesses.

Finally, make sure the first release can grow. The apps and the backend should be built so that adding payment methods, dispatch rules or a second city is an extension, not a rewrite. That is a conversation to have with whoever builds the product before work starts.
