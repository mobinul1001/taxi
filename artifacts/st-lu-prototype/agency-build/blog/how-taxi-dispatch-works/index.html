How Taxi Dispatch Works: Manual, Auto-Assign and Zones

The three ways a job gets to a driver, what each is good at, and how to pick the one that fits your fleet.

By TaxiAppDevelopment.org TeamPublished Nov 24, 2026Updated Nov 24, 2026 5 min read

On this page

  1. What dispatch actually does
  2. Manual dispatch
  3. Auto-assign to the nearest driver
  4. Zone queues

Key takeaways

  • Dispatch is the rule that decides which driver gets which job.
  • Manual dispatch gives control, auto-assign gives speed, zones give order.
  • Most operators mix them: automatic by default, manual for special jobs.
  • Start with the simplest rule that works and add rules when real problems appear.

What dispatch actually does

Every taxi booking is a request that needs an owner. Someone has to decide which driver takes it, tell that driver, and know what happens if the driver says no. That decision is dispatch. In a small firm it is a person with a phone. In a software platform it is a set of rules, with a person watching over them.

The rule matters more than it looks. It decides how long a passenger waits, how fairly work is shared between drivers, how far drivers drive empty, and how much of your dispatcher's day is spent on routine jobs instead of problems.

Manual dispatch

In manual dispatch a dispatcher sees the queue of jobs and picks a driver for each one. The console shows who is free, where they are and what vehicle they drive, and the dispatcher assigns with a click.

This suits airport transfers, tours and corporate trips, where a job is planned in advance and the right driver or vehicle matters more than speed. It also suits small fleets, where the dispatcher knows every driver.

  • Strength: full control, and good judgement for unusual jobs.
  • Weakness: it does not scale. Every job costs a person some attention.
  • Weakness: the quality of the day depends on who is on shift.

Auto-assign to the nearest driver

Auto-assign sends a new job to the closest available driver, usually by straight distance or by estimated arrival time on the road. The driver gets a request and accepts or declines. If the driver declines or does not answer in time, the job moves to the next driver.

This is the model passengers know from ride-hailing apps. It is fast and needs no dispatcher for routine jobs, which is why it fits on-demand city rides.

  • Strength: short waits and no manual work for normal jobs.
  • Weakness: drivers who sit in busy areas get most of the work, so you may need fairness rules.
  • Weakness: it knows distance, not context. It will not know a VIP pickup needs your best car.

Zone queues

In a zone model the map is divided into areas, and drivers join a queue for the zone they are in. The first driver in the queue gets the next job from that zone. Airports, ranks and busy hotels are the classic cases.

Zones make fair turn-taking visible, and drivers understand them without explanation. The cost is setup. Someone has to draw the zones, keep them current and decide what happens at the edges.

  • Strength: fair, predictable and easy to explain to drivers.
  • Weakness: needs maintenance as the city and your business change.

Mixing the three

Most real operations combine them. A common setup is auto-assign for ordinary on-demand rides, a zone queue at the airport, and manual assignment for scheduled and corporate jobs. The dispatcher console then becomes the place where exceptions are handled: a driver who declines three times, a passenger who is waiting too long, a job nobody has accepted.

Whatever you choose, give the dispatcher an override. Rules are good at the normal case, and people are good at the case nobody planned for.

What the rules look at

Even a simple dispatch rule needs inputs. The most common is distance or estimated arrival time between the driver and the pickup. Others are whether the driver is free, what type of vehicle the job needs, how long the driver has been waiting, and whether the driver has recently declined jobs.

Each extra input makes the rule fairer or smarter, and also harder to explain. A good habit is to write the rule in one plain sentence. If you cannot say it simply to a driver, it is probably too complicated for the first version.

  • Distance or arrival time to the pickup
  • Driver status: free, busy or offline
  • Vehicle type and number of seats
  • Time since the driver's last job
  • Recent declines or missed requests

Timeouts, declines and fallbacks

Every automatic rule needs an answer to the question: what if the driver does not respond? A typical flow sends the request to one driver for a short time, perhaps fifteen to thirty seconds, and then moves on to the next. After a set number of attempts, the job goes to the dispatcher as an exception.

This fallback is the part that protects the passenger. Without it, a job can quietly sit in a queue while the passenger waits. With it, someone is told as soon as the system cannot place a job, and can phone a driver or take over by hand.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is building complicated rules before you have real jobs to learn from. The second is hiding the rule from drivers, who then suspect favouritism. A short written explanation of how jobs are offered builds trust.

The third is forgetting the dispatcher. Even a highly automatic system needs a console that shows jobs, drivers and problems at a glance, with one-click assignment as a safety net. The last is ignoring the data. Look at how long jobs wait, how often drivers decline and where jobs go unassigned, and adjust the rule from what you see.

How to choose

Ask four questions. How many jobs are booked in advance versus on demand? How many drivers do you have, and does the dispatcher know them? Do you have places where drivers queue? And how costly is a late pickup for your customers?

If most jobs are planned, lean on manual. If most are on demand in one city, lean on auto-assign. If you run an airport or rank, add zones. Then start with the simplest rule that works and watch real jobs for a few weeks before adding more. Complicated rules built before you have data are usually wrong.
