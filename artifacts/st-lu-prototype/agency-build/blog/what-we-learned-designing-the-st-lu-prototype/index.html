What We Learned Designing the St Lu Prototype

Notes from designing a passenger app, a driver app and a dispatcher panel as one connected product.

Key takeaways

  • St Lu is a clickable design prototype, not a live product.
  • One shared set of job states keeps all three screens consistent.
  • A prototype lets an owner react to real screens before anything is built.
  • Showing the dispatcher what the passenger sees prevents most confusion.

What the prototype is

St Lu Taxi & Tours is a taxi, airport transfer and tours business in St. Lucia. The prototype is a clickable design of a platform for it: a passenger app for booking, a driver app for jobs, and a dispatcher panel for assigning work. It runs in a browser with sample data. It has no live backend and takes no real payments, so it shows a product direction and not live results.

This post shares the design thinking behind it. It is a design project, and we say so plainly.

One set of job states

The most useful decision was to define the life of a job once and use it everywhere. A job is new, then assigned, then accepted, then on the way, arrived, in progress and completed. The passenger sees these as friendly status messages. The driver sees them as buttons to tap. The dispatcher sees them as columns and badges.

When all three screens describe the same states, nobody has to translate between them. It also makes the prototype easy to explain: the same job, three views.

Three people, three views of the same trip

Each role needs different things from the same trip. The passenger wants to know when the car arrives. The driver wants the pickup, the destination and one clear next action. The dispatcher wants every job and every driver at once, with the problems at the top.

We designed from the job outwards, asking what each person needs to see at each state, rather than designing three separate apps.

Own drivers and outside drivers

A tours business often uses its own drivers and also partners when it is busy. The dispatcher panel therefore lets the operator assign a job to an in-house driver or to an outside one. Treating both as normal, instead of as an exception, kept the dispatcher's day simpler and the screens honest about how the business works.

Designing the booking flow

The passenger flow starts from what the business sells: airport transfers, tours and taxi rides. Each is a different kind of booking, so the first choice a passenger makes is what they want to book, and the screens that follow adapt to it.

We kept the flow short. Where am I going, when, in what vehicle and how will I pay. A passenger should reach a confirmed booking without having to read instructions, and the booking screen should show the fare before asking for payment.

Designing for the dispatcher

A dispatcher works under time pressure, so the panel puts the jobs that need action first, shows driver availability beside each job and makes assignment a single deliberate action. A live map and a trip timeline give context when a job needs attention.

The aim was a screen a dispatcher can read in seconds. That meant fewer decorations, clear labels and consistent colours for the same status wherever it appears.

Letting a brand lead the visuals

St Lu has its own orange brand. In the prototype the passenger app, the driver app and the dispatcher panel all use it, so the product feels like the operator's own and not a generic template.

That also shows a point for any business: a branded prototype is more convincing to the owner and to drivers than grey wireframes, because they can picture it with their own name on it.

What comes after a prototype

A prototype answers design questions and leaves engineering questions open. The next stage for a project like this would be to agree a first-release scope from what the prototype shows, choose the payment provider and mapping services, define how jobs and drivers are stored, and plan testing with a small group of real drivers.

It is also the point to check the numbers. With a scope in hand, a written estimate can be made against something specific. That is the main reason we build prototypes first: the budget conversation is about screens everybody has already seen.

What the prototype does not show

It is worth being clear about the limits. The prototype uses sample data, so it does not show real waiting times, real fares or real driver behaviour. It has no live payments, no real maps and no real notifications.

It also cannot show how the business performs, because there is no live business behind it. Treat it as a way to agree what to build, not as proof that it will work. Only a real launch with real drivers can show that.

Why a prototype first

A clickable prototype lets the owner and drivers react to real screens before any backend exists. Disagreements about the booking flow or the dispatch rules show up early, when they cost a conversation and not a rebuild.

It also gives everyone a shared reference when the scope is written. If the prototype does something, it can be listed. If it does not, it can be discussed.
